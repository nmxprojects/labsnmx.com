Reading YouTube Analytics: five numbers and a 30-minute weekly routine

YouTube Studio shows you dozens of charts. Five of them answer almost every question a small channel has. Here they are, what “good” looks like, and the weekly routine that turns them into decisions.

By Anisha, NMX Labs3 November 20266 min read

Quick summary

  • Impressions click-through rate tells you whether the title and thumbnail work.
  • Average view duration and the retention graph tell you whether the video works.
  • Traffic sources tell you how people found you; returning viewers tell you whether they came back.
  • Compare each video with your own recent videos, not with other channels, and change one thing at a time.

YouTube Studio can feel like an aeroplane cockpit. The good news is that a small channel only needs five numbers, looked at regularly, to make better videos. Here they are, in the order we read them.

1. Impressions click-through rate

Out of everyone who saw your thumbnail on YouTube, how many clicked? This is mostly about your title and thumbnail. A low rate on a video with plenty of impressions says the packaging isn’t working; a high rate with few impressions says the packaging works but YouTube hasn’t shown it widely yet. Rates vary a lot by topic and where the impressions come from, so judge it against your own videos rather than a magic target.

2. Average view duration

How long, on average, people watched. Read it alongside the video’s length: four minutes of a five-minute video is very different from four minutes of a forty-minute one. If people click but leave early, the title and thumbnail made a promise the video didn’t keep quickly enough.

3. The audience retention graph

This is the most useful chart in Studio. It shows, second by second, how much of your audience is still watching.

  • A steep drop in the first 30 seconds: your opening is slow or doesn’t match the title.
  • A sudden dip in the middle: something there lost people: a tangent, a long pause, an ad-like section.
  • Bumps upward: people rewatched that part. Make more of whatever happens there.

4. Traffic sources

How people found the video: YouTube search, browse features (the home page and subscriptions), suggested videos (next to other videos), the Shorts feed, or outside links such as WhatsApp and Instagram. Search-heavy videos keep getting found for months; suggested-heavy videos ride on the back of related content. Knowing which kind you’re making tells you how to title it.

5. Returning viewers

Studio shows how many viewers are new and how many have watched you before. Returning viewers are the closest thing to a loyal audience. If the number grows, people like what you make; if almost everyone is new every time, work on series, playlists and end screens that lead to the next video.

The 30-minute weekly routine

Pick a fixed time, say Monday morning, and do the same five steps:

  1. Look at each video from the last few weeks at the same age, for example seven days after upload, so comparisons are fair.
  2. Write down the five numbers in a simple sheet: one row per video.
  3. Open the retention graph of your best and worst video and note what happens at the biggest drop and the biggest bump.
  4. Read the comments for questions people asked. Those are next month’s topics.
  5. Choose one change for the next video: a faster opening, a different thumbnail style, a tighter middle. Only one, so you can tell whether it worked.

After two months your sheet becomes the most valuable thing your channel owns: a record of what your audience actually responds to. That’s worth more than any general advice, including ours.
