The rules to follow on YouTube: guidelines, copyright, AI and paid promotions

Most channels that get into trouble didn’t mean to break anything. Here are the rules that catch new creators in India most often, explained in plain language, and what to do instead.

By Neeraj, NMX Labs15 September 20267 min read

Quick summary

  • Community Guidelines strikes expire after 90 days; three active strikes end a channel.
  • A Content ID claim and a copyright strike are different things. Use music you have the rights to.
  • Reused or mass-produced content can’t earn money, even if it’s allowed to stay up.
  • Disclose realistic AI-made content and paid promotions, both on YouTube and under Indian advertising rules.

Nobody starts a channel planning to break the rules. But we regularly meet creators whose videos were taken down, demonetised or blocked in some countries for reasons they didn’t know existed. This lesson covers the rules that trip up new creators most, in plain language. It isn’t legal advice, and YouTube updates its policies often, so check the YouTube Help Centre before you rely on any detail.

Community Guidelines and strikes

The Community Guidelines cover things like harmful or dangerous content, harassment, hate, misleading medical claims, spam and scams. If a video breaks them, YouTube removes it. The first time, you usually get a warning. After that, each violation is a strike. Strikes expire after 90 days, and three active strikes at the same time end the channel. Read the email YouTube sends: it names the policy, which tells you what to avoid next time.

Copyright: claims are not the same as strikes

This is the one that confuses most people.

  • A Content ID claim happens automatically when YouTube recognises someone else’s music or footage in your video. The owner can choose to earn the ad money from your video, track it, or block it in some countries. Your channel isn’t penalised, but you may lose the earnings.
  • A copyright strike happens when an owner formally asks YouTube to take your video down. Like other strikes, three active ones end a channel.

“I only used 10 seconds” and “I wrote ‘no copyright intended’” protect you from neither. India’s copyright law has exceptions for things like criticism and review, but they are narrower than most people assume and are judged case by case. The safe route is simple: use YouTube’s free Audio Library, music you’ve licensed, or your own.

Reused and mass-produced content

Some videos are allowed on YouTube but can’t earn money. YouTube’s monetisation rules exclude content that mostly reuses other people’s material without meaningful commentary, and what it now calls inauthentic content: in July 2025 it renamed its old “repetitious content” rule, and in July 2026 it spelled out three kinds that can’t be monetised. They are generic or repetitive template videos that look mass-produced, content made to upset or manipulate viewers for views, and AI personas presented as experts on sensitive topics like health and money. Reaction compilations with no reaction, slideshows with the same robotic narration and re-uploaded clips all fall on the wrong side.

AI-made content needs a label when it looks real

Using AI tools isn’t against the rules. But if your video contains realistic content made or meaningfully changed with AI (a cloned voice of a real person, a generated scene that looks like real footage), you have to tick the AI disclosure when you upload. YouTube then adds a label. YouTube says the label itself doesn’t reduce reach or earnings. Not disclosing can.

Paid promotions: disclose twice

If a brand paid you, sent you a free product or you have an affiliate link, tick “includes paid promotion” in YouTube Studio. In India you also have to follow the Advertising Standards Council of India’s influencer guidelines, which ask for a clear label such as “Ad” or “Collaboration” that viewers can’t miss, and the government’s endorsement rules for influencers say the same. Saying it out loud in the video and writing it at the top of the description covers you.

Two settings people forget

  • Made for kids. Every upload has to say whether it’s made for children. It changes which features the video gets, so answer honestly.
  • Thumbnails and titles. Misleading or shocking thumbnails can be removed under the spam and deceptive practices rules, not just ignored by viewers.

A checklist before you publish

  1. Do I own or have rights to every piece of music, footage and image?
  2. Does the video add my own commentary, teaching or story?
  3. Does any realistic part come from AI? Disclose it.
  4. Was anything paid for or gifted? Disclose it in the video, the description and the Studio setting.
  5. Are the title and thumbnail honest about what’s inside?
