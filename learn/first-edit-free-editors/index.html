Your first edit: free editors that work in India, and cuts that keep people watching

CapCut has been blocked in India since 2020, so here’s what to use instead on phones and computers, plus the five edits that matter more than any effect.

By Neeraj, NMX Labs6 October 20266 min read

Quick summary

  • CapCut is banned in India. VN, DaVinci Resolve, Clipchamp and iMovie are good free options.
  • Cut silences, false starts and slow intros: that’s most of what “editing” means.
  • Add captions and on-screen text; many people watch with the sound off.
  • Keep music well below your voice, and export at the resolution you shot in.

Editing scares new creators more than filming does. Timelines, keyframes, transitions… it looks like a profession. For your first twenty videos, though, editing mostly means one thing: removing the parts that make people leave. Here are the free tools that work in India and the handful of edits that actually matter.

First, a note on CapCut

CapCut is the editor most international tutorials recommend. It has been blocked in India since June 2020, along with other apps the government banned, and that ban is still in place. Install-from-anywhere workarounds put your device and data at risk, so we don’t recommend them. You don’t need it.

Free editors worth learning

  • VN Video Editor (Android and iPhone): a proper multi-track editor on your phone, with no watermark on the free version at the time of writing. The closest thing to what most CapCut tutorials show.
  • YouTube Create (Android, where available): YouTube’s own free editing app, with captions, voice-over and music that’s cleared for YouTube.
  • DaVinci Resolve (Windows, Mac, Linux): a professional editor whose free version is enough for almost any YouTube channel. It needs a reasonably powerful computer and some patience to learn.
  • Clipchamp (Windows, and in the browser): simple, and already installed on many Windows 11 laptops.
  • iMovie (iPhone and Mac): free, simple, good enough for talking-head videos.

Pick one and stick with it for a month. Switching editors every week is how people stay beginners.

The five edits that matter

1. Cut the start

Watch your first 30 seconds. Most first drafts take 15 seconds to get going: settling in, “so…”, a long hello. Delete it and start on the hook. This single cut often improves retention more than everything else combined.

2. Remove silences and retakes

Cut the gaps between sentences, the false starts and the “let me say that again”. These jump cuts look normal on YouTube; viewers don’t mind them, they mind waiting.

3. Cover the talking with what you’re talking about

When you mention a product, a screen or a place, cut to it. These cutaways (B-roll) also hide jump cuts.

4. Add text

Captions for everything you say, or at least on-screen text for key points and numbers. Many viewers watch on mute, and text helps anyone listening in a second language.

5. Get the sound level right

Your voice should be clear and consistent. Background music should sit well under it, low enough that you’d notice if it stopped but never have to strain to hear words. Use music from YouTube’s Audio Library or another source you have rights to.

Exporting

Export at the resolution and frame rate you filmed in, usually 1080p at 30 frames per second. MP4 with H.264 video and AAC audio is the safe, widely supported choice for YouTube. If the file is huge, that’s fine: YouTube recompresses everything anyway, and a better source file looks better after upload.

Effects can wait

Transitions, zooms, sound effects and animated titles are fun, and later they can give your channel a style. But they don’t fix a slow video. Get the five edits above right on ten videos, then add one effect at a time and see whether people watch longer.
