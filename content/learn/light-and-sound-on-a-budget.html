If you take one idea from this lesson, take this: cameras record light and microphones record rooms. Improve the light and the room, and every device you own gets better at once. Here’s how to do both cheaply, with the things you probably have at home.

Light

Big and soft beats small and bright

A small light source (a bare bulb, a phone torch) throws hard shadows under your eyes and nose. A big source (a window, a white wall with light bouncing off it) wraps around your face and looks flattering. That’s the whole secret of “soft light”, and it’s why expensive studio lights come with big diffusers.

The window setup

  1. Sit facing a window, or turn so the window is at about 45 degrees to one side.
  2. If the sun shines straight in and makes harsh patches, hang a thin white curtain or bedsheet over the window. Now the whole window glows evenly.
  3. If one side of your face is too dark, put something white on that side: a chart paper, a white bedsheet over a chair, a foam board. It bounces light back for free.

Don’t mix colours

Daylight is bluish, most home bulbs are yellow or a cool tube-light white. When two of them hit your face, the camera can’t balance both, and you end up half orange, half blue. Pick one: switch off room lights when you use the window, or close the curtains when you use lamps.

When you do buy a light

A small LED panel or ring light helps once you film after dark. Look for adjustable brightness and colour, and bounce it off a wall or diffuse it rather than pointing it straight at your face.

Sound

Fix the room first

Echo comes from hard, empty surfaces: tiles, bare walls, glass. Soft things absorb it. That’s why a bedroom with curtains and a bed sounds better than an empty hall, and why some creators famously record next to a cupboard full of clothes. Add cushions, a rug or a blanket on the floor out of shot and you’ll hear the difference.

Kill the noise you’ve stopped hearing

Ceiling fans, coolers, the fridge, the AC and traffic all end up in your recording, and they’re hard to remove later. Switch off what you can for the takes. Close the windows. Record at the quietest time of day for your street.

Distance is everything

A microphone close to your mouth hears more of you and less of the room. A phone on a tripod two metres away picks up the room; a clip-on mic on your collar picks up you. If you only buy one thing for your channel, make it a decent clip-on microphone.

Two habits from professional sets

  • Record room tone. Before you start, stay silent for 10 seconds. Editing apps use it to reduce background noise, and you can use it to fill gaps between cuts.
  • Listen on headphones. Phone speakers hide hum, echo and clipping. Do a 20-second test recording and listen with earphones before you film the whole thing.

The order to spend money

Room fixes (free), a clip-on mic, then a light, then, much later, a camera. Most viewers will never notice your camera. They notice immediately when they can’t see your face or hear your words.
