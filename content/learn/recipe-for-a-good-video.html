Pick any three videos you finished recently without skipping. A cooking video, a phone review, a history explainer. They look nothing alike, but they almost certainly did the same three things: they told you very quickly why you should care, they told you what you’d get, and then they gave you exactly that. We call it hook, promise, payoff, and it’s the first thing we check in every script.

1. The hook: why stay?

The hook is the first few seconds. Its only job is to make the right person think “this is for me”. The strongest hooks name a problem the viewer already has (“Your Reels look blurry after upload. Here’s why.”) or show the result first (the finished dish, the before-and-after, the final number).

What doesn’t belong in the hook: a logo animation, “Hi guys, welcome back to my channel”, your life story, or a request to subscribe. All of those can come later, or never.

2. The promise: what will I get?

Right after the hook, say plainly what the viewer will be able to do by the end. “By the end of this video you’ll have a lighting setup that costs nothing and works in any room.” This one sentence does two things. It tells the viewer the video is worth their time, and it tells you, the creator, what the rest of the video must deliver. If a section doesn’t serve the promise, cut it.

3. The payoff: deliver it, fully

This is the body of the video, and the place where most channels lose trust. If the title says “5 settings”, show five settings, clearly, in order. Show rather than tell wherever you can: the screen, the product, your hands. Every time you say “for example”, the viewer should see the example.

Two habits keep people watching through the middle:

  • One idea per section. Finish a point before you start the next.
  • Change something every so often. A new angle, a close-up, on-screen text or a quick cutaway resets attention without any fancy editing.

The ending: point somewhere

Once the promise is delivered, stop. Don’t recap everything. Point to one related video (“If your problem is sound rather than light, watch this one next”) and use YouTube’s end screen to link it. A viewer who watches two of your videos is far more likely to come back than one who watches one.

A worked example

Topic: making a Reel look sharp after upload.

  • Hook (0:00): side-by-side of a blurry and a sharp Reel. “Same video, same phone. One setting is the difference.”
  • Promise (0:08): “In three minutes you’ll know the export and upload settings that keep your videos sharp.”
  • Payoff (0:15): the camera setting, the export setting, the upload setting, each shown on screen.
  • End (2:50): “Blurry is fixed. If your problem is shaky footage, this video is next.”

Write the promise first

Before you open a script document, write one sentence: “By the end of this video you’ll be able to…” If you can’t finish that sentence, you don’t have a video yet, you have a topic. Keep working on it until the sentence is specific. Everything else, the hook, the order of sections, the title and even the thumbnail, gets easier once that sentence is clear.
