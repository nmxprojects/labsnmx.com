Educational content is some of the most rewarding work on YouTube. People search for answers every day, a good explainer can keep finding viewers for years, and you don’t need to perform or chase trends. But teaching on camera is different from teaching in a classroom. Nobody can raise a hand, and leaving takes one swipe. Here’s how we approach explainers so people stay until the end.

Start with one learning goal

Write down what the viewer will be able to do after watching. “Understand GST” is too big. “File a GST return for a small online shop without a CA” is a video. If your outline has five big ideas, you probably have five videos. Make them, link them, and put them in a playlist. Each one gets its own clear title, which also helps search.

Pick the format that fits the subject

Talking head

You, facing the camera, explaining. Best for opinions, stories, career advice and concepts. It builds trust fastest, but it gets tiring if nothing changes on screen, so cut to examples, text or images regularly.

Screen recording

Best for software, apps, spreadsheets, websites and anything with clicks. On a computer, OBS Studio is free and reliable. On phones, the built-in screen recorder works. Increase the zoom or text size so it’s readable on a phone, and move the mouse slowly. A small circle of your face in a corner keeps it personal.

Hands-on, from above

Best for cooking, crafts, maths on paper, unboxing and repairs. Mount the phone looking straight down using an overhead stand, or a tripod with an arm. Keep your hands and the object filling the frame.

Whiteboard or tablet

Best for diagrams, maths and processes. A cheap whiteboard on a wall works, or a tablet with a drawing app recorded on screen. Write large and stand to the side so you don’t block it.

Show every example

The fastest way to lose a learner is to describe something they can’t see. Every time you say “for example” or “like this”, the screen should show it: the spreadsheet cell, the finished dough, the error message. Plan these shots before you film, as a simple two-column list: what you say, what we see.

Production habits that keep people watching

  • Say the outcome early. “In six minutes you’ll be able to…” sets the deal.
  • Use on-screen text for key terms and numbers. People watching on mute or learning in a second language will thank you.
  • Turn on captions and check them. YouTube’s automatic captions are a good start but misread names and technical words; correcting them takes a few minutes in YouTube Studio.
  • Recap in one breath at the end, then point to the next lesson.

Add chapters

Chapters let learners jump to the part they need, and they appear in search results. To add them, list timestamps in your description, starting with 00:00, with at least three chapters, each at least 10 seconds long. Give each chapter a plain name: “00:00 What you need”, “01:10 Setting up the sheet”, and so on.

Language

Teach in the language your learners think in. Many Indian educational channels grew fastest in Hindi, Hinglish or a regional language, because they answered questions nobody was answering in that language. If you’re comfortable in more than one, it’s worth testing.
