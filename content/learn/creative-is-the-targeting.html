When I started running Meta ads, targeting was a craft. You stacked interests, excluded lookalikes and felt like a genius. Then the system got better at finding customers than we were, and the craft moved somewhere else: into the ad.

The auction, in plain English

Every time someone scrolls past an ad slot, Meta runs an auction. The winner isn’t simply whoever bids most. Meta combines three things: your bid, the estimated action rate (how likely this person is to do what you want) and ad quality. A cheaper ad that people actually want to see can beat an expensive one they scroll past.

Why creative became targeting

With broad targeting or Advantage+ audiences, you give Meta a wide pool and it decides who sees what. How does it decide? Largely by watching who stops, taps and buys after seeing your ad. A video about saving time attracts different people from one about saving money, even with identical targeting settings. Meta’s ad retrieval system, which it calls Andromeda, leans on exactly these signals.

So the useful question isn’t “which interests?” any more. It’s “which ideas?”. Three genuinely different concepts (a problem, a proof, an offer) will reach three different groups of people. Five colour variations of the same concept will mostly reach one.

The learning phase, and why small budgets should stay simple

After you launch or significantly change an ad set, it goes through a learning phase. Meta’s guidance is that an ad set needs around 50 optimisation events (purchases, leads, whatever you optimise for) within 7 days to exit it. Results are less stable until then.

Do the maths for a small budget. If a sale costs you ₹800 and you spend ₹1,000 a day, you get about nine sales a week per ad set. Split that budget across five ad sets and none of them ever finishes learning. So:

  • Use fewer ad sets with more budget each.
  • If purchases are too rare, optimise for an earlier event, like add to cart or a lead, while you build volume.
  • Put the variety in your ads, not in your audience settings.
