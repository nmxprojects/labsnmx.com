Why CRED made Rahul Dravid smash a car mirror: the against-type casting playbook

In April 2021, CRED took the calmest man in Indian cricket and had him scream at traffic. The ad became the most talked-about of that IPL. Here’s the psychology behind casting against type, its risks, and how a brand without a celebrity budget can borrow it.

By Anisha, NMX Labs · Market Research Lead21 November 20266 min read

Quick summary

  • CRED’s April 2021 IPL ad cast Rahul Dravid, famous for his calm, as a road-raging “Indiranagar ka gunda”. It was reported as the most viral ad of IPL 2021.

For two decades Rahul Dravid was the most dependable thing in Indian cricket. His nickname was “The Wall”. He didn’t sledge, he didn’t sulk and he certainly didn’t lose his temper. So when CRED released an ad on 9 April 2021 in which Dravid, stuck in Bengaluru traffic, smashes a car mirror and yells from his sunroof that he’s the gangster of Indiranagar, the internet didn’t quite believe what it was watching.

Virat Kohli tweeted that he’d never seen that side of Dravid. Within hours, “Indiranagar ka gunda” had turned into a meme, and by the end of the season a report on IPL advertising named it the most viral ad of IPL 2021. It’s now a standard case study, but the interesting question isn’t whether it worked. It’s why, and whether a brand without a cricket legend can use the same idea.

The mechanism: a broken expectation

We all carry mental shortcuts about famous people. Dravid equals patience, restraint, reliability. Advertising usually uses those shortcuts directly: a calm cricketer endorses a dependable bank. CRED did the opposite and broke the shortcut on purpose.

Psychologists who study advertising call this schema incongruity: when something doesn’t match what we expect, we pay more attention and work harder to make sense of it. Mild incongruity is enjoyable, like a joke. That extra processing is what makes it memorable, and the urge to show someone else is what makes it shareable. The ad didn’t need to explain itself. Everyone already knew the joke.

There was a second, local layer. Indiranagar is the Bengaluru neighbourhood where Dravid grew up, and it was also where CRED had its office. The line carried a small wink to the city that made it land harder there and travel further everywhere else.

It was a series, not a one-off

The Dravid ad belonged to CRED’s “Not Everyone Gets It” campaigns, which repeatedly cast well-known faces in unexpected roles, including Jackie Shroff as a Zumba enthusiast and Kapil Dev playing Ranveer Singh, before moving on to cartoon characters from 90s comics. The repeated structure is what turned a viral moment into a brand style. Like Amul’s topicals, the format stayed fixed while the surprise changed.

The ripple: other brands did CRED’s distribution for free

On launch day, brand accounts piled in. Zomato joked that deliveries in Indiranagar might run late because of an angry gunda on the road. Cars24 offered upgrades to anyone whose car he’d damaged. Each reply pushed the original ad into another audience at no cost to CRED. That’s the hidden economics of a genuinely shareable idea: when it’s strong enough, your competitors and neighbours become your media plan.

The risks nobody puts in the case study

Remembering the joke, forgetting the brand

The classic failure of entertaining ads is that people recall the gag and not who paid for it. CRED’s ads have often been criticised for being better known than the product. If your brand isn’t unmistakable, ideally visible early and repeated at the end, the surprise works for the celebrity, not for you.

Wearing out the trick

Against-type casting depends on surprise. Once audiences expect the inversion, it stops being surprising. Long-running campaigns need new kinds of surprise, which is why CRED moved from celebrities to comics.

The celebrity’s own reputation

The joke only works if it’s affectionate. Dravid could play the gunda because his real reputation was so secure that nobody believed it. He later joked that his mother still wasn’t convinced. A celebrity with a fragile or contested image is a much riskier bet.

How to use this without a celebrity budget

You don’t need Rahul Dravid to use the mechanism. You need something your audience has a strong expectation about, and the confidence to flip it.

  • Invert your category’s cliché. If every skincare ad shows flawless skin, show the 7 am reality. If every fintech ad is earnest, be the one that jokes. The expectation you break can belong to a whole category.
  • Cast your founder against type. A serious founder doing something silly, or a playful founder being deadly serious about one thing, works on social video at almost no cost.
  • Keep the product in the frame. The surprise should happen to or around the product, so the memory includes it.
  • Test before you scale. Run two or three versions as short Meta ads and see which one people watch to the end and share. Our creative testing system explains how we structure that, and why creative variety now drives targeting.

The takeaway

CRED’s Dravid ad worked because it spent a reputation someone else had spent 25 years building, in 30 seconds, in a way that made that reputation stronger rather than weaker. The cheap lesson isn’t “hire a legend”. It’s that the most valuable thing in an ad is often an expectation your audience already holds, and you can choose to break it.
