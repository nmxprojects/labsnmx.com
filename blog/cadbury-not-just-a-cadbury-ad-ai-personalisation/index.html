Cadbury’s “Not Just a Cadbury Ad”: the AI personalisation campaign that came too early

Years before every app could clone a voice, Cadbury made Shah Rukh Khan say the names of thousands of neighbourhood shops. It won India’s first Titanium Lion. Taken apart, it’s still the best brief for AI personalisation an Indian brand has written.

By Neeraj, NMX Labs · AI Design Expert19 November 20266 min readFact-checked 22 November 2026
Summarise with Google AI (opens Google AI Mode in a new tab)

Key takeaways

  • In 2020 Cadbury Celebrations gave its Diwali ad space to local shops. In 2021 it went further, using machine learning to recreate Shah Rukh Khan’s face and voice so he named individual stores.
  • Store owners generated their own ads on a microsite; the film came in four versions for four store categories, according to the awards entry.
  • It won India’s first Titanium Lion in 2022 and the Creative Effectiveness Grand Prix in 2023. Rephrase.ai, the AI partner, was bought by Adobe in November 2023.
  • In 2026 the technology is cheap. The hard parts are now the ones Cadbury solved first: a generous idea, the celebrity’s consent, a tight script and clear disclosure.

Diwali is the most expensive time of year to advertise in India and, for neighbourhood shops, often the most important time to sell. In 2020, with small businesses battered by the pandemic, Cadbury Celebrations did something unusual with its festive ad: it handed part of it over to them. The next year it went much further, and made the country’s biggest film star say the names of their shops.

The campaign is remembered as an early deepfake ad. That undersells it. It’s one of the clearest examples of AI personalisation built around a generous idea, and it was finished years before the tools to make it became cheap.

2020: give the ad away

The first version didn’t use any AI. Cadbury’s idea was to share its advertising space with local stores, a hyper-local campaign that promoted neighbourhood shops instead of only the chocolate1. It established the thing that matters most in this story: the brand was doing a favour for someone other than itself.

2021: make the star say your shop’s name

For Diwali 2021, the team asked a bigger question. What if Shah Rukh Khan, already Cadbury’s ambassador, could become the ambassador for every small shop near you? Working with Rephrase.ai and DeltaX, they used machine learning to recreate his face and voice so that he could name individual stores in the ad1.

Shop owners went to a microsite, entered their store’s details, and got their own version of the film to share on social media and messaging apps2. The film came in four versions, one each for fashion, footwear, general and electronics stores, so that what he said made sense for the shop being promoted3. The microsite launched publicly in late October 20218.

The ads were then targeted locally, the part of the work that later won a Gold Lion for data-driven targeting6. Every film worked twice: once as a Cadbury ad and once as an ad for a business that could never have afforded a film star.

The results, and how to read them

At Cannes Lions 2022 the campaign won a Titanium Lion, the first for India513, along with Gold Lions in Creative Data and Direct6. A year later it took the Creative Effectiveness Grand Prix in the market disruption category, which judges results as well as the idea7. Ogilvy’s global chief executive, Devika Bulchandani, called it an example of “personalisation at scale”11.

The effectiveness entry describes an unprecedented 35% business growth4. Treat that the way you’d treat any awards entry: the figure is the entrant’s own, and the entry doesn’t define exactly what was measured. What’s beyond dispute is the reach of the idea, which became one of the most-cited Indian campaigns of the decade.

Two years after the campaign, in November 2023, Adobe bought Rephrase.ai. Reports described it as Adobe’s first acquisition of an Indian start-up and its first of a generative AI company, with most of the team joining Adobe910.

We took it apart

How we checked this

We rebuilt the campaign’s structure from the agency case study and awards entries, then wrote the same structure as a template a smaller brand could use. Nothing here is from Cadbury’s internal files.

LayerWhat Cadbury didWhy it mattered
IdeaLend the brand’s star to small shops1Gave people a reason to share it that wasn’t about chocolate
Fixed scriptOne film, the same structure for everyoneOnly the shop details changed, so quality stayed under control
VariantsFour store categories3Enough relevance without an unmanageable number of versions
SlotThe shop’s name and detailsThe single personal element, which is what made people share it
Self-serve toolA microsite for owners2Thousands of ads without an agency team making each one
DistributionLocal, data-driven targeting6The right film reached the right street

The template for a D2C brand: one 20-second film; three variants (for example, by city tier or by retail type); one slot (a stockist’s name, a city or a customer’s first name); a simple form that produces the file; and a rule that every variant is reviewed by sampling before it goes out. The fewer slots, the safer and better it looks.

Why we say it came too early

In 2021 this needed a specialist start-up, custom machine learning, a celebrity willing to have his face and voice modelled, and an agency able to coordinate all of it. Very few brands could attempt it. Today, voice cloning, lip-sync and avatar tools are sold by the month, and a version of this campaign is technically within reach of a mid-sized brand.

What hasn’t got cheaper is everything around the technology. The idea still has to be generous rather than gimmicky. The person whose face or voice is used still has to agree, in writing, to every use. Audiences are now far more aware of synthetic video, and platforms increasingly label it; Meta, for example, can add an “AI info” label to ads made or edited with AI, which we covered here. Cadbury succeeded because the idea did something people valued and nobody felt tricked. That part was never about the model.

Cadbury kept the series going, too, with other personalised festive work, including messages featuring Hrithik Roshan12. The approach outlived the novelty.

If you want to try this in 2026

Start with who benefits

Cadbury’s campaign worked because the shopkeeper got something. Ask who gets value from your personalised ad besides you: a stockist, a delivery partner, a customer celebrating something.

Get consent that covers the use

If you use a real person, including a founder or an employee, get written permission that covers synthetic versions, every channel and a time limit. If you can’t, use a voice-over artist or an illustrated character instead.

Personalise one thing, not five

Each extra slot multiplies the chance of a weird result. Cadbury’s main slot was the store. Yours can be a name, a city or an occasion.

Say it’s AI

Disclose the technique in the caption or the film. It costs you nothing and protects the trust that makes the idea work.

Measure what the partner gets

If the idea is to help someone else, measure that too. Count how many partners generated and shared their version, and ask a sample of them whether it brought customers in. A campaign that is generous on paper but useless to the people it features won’t travel the way Cadbury’s did.

Review by sampling

Before launch, have a person watch a random sample of generated versions, including the strangest inputs you can think of. The one embarrassing version is the one that gets screenshotted.

The bottom line

Most AI personalisation is a brand saying your name back to you. Cadbury’s was a brand saying someone else’s name, a shop that needed the help, and that’s why it travelled. The tools that made it remarkable in 2021 are ordinary now. The brief that made it work is still rare.
