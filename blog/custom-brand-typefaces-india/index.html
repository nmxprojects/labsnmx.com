Custom brand typefaces in India: why apps commission their own fonts

Your font is the voice your brand uses on every screen, price tag and ad. In India it also has to speak several scripts and print a rupee sign. We tested ten popular fonts to see how many can, and the answer explains why big brands commission their own.

By Anisha, NMX Labs · Market Research Lead22 November 20266 min readFact-checked 22 November 2026
Summarise with Google AI (opens Google AI Mode in a new tab)

Key takeaways

  • In our test of ten popular Latin fonts, nine had no Devanagari, Bengali, Tamil or Gujarati characters at all, and two (Open Sans and Archivo Black) lacked the ₹ sign.
  • When a font lacks a script, the phone silently swaps in a different one. Your Hindi ad then appears in a typeface you never chose, with a different weight and personality.
  • That gap is one reason Indian companies commission custom, multi-script fonts, as Air India did with Air India Sans in 2023, and why Indian foundries do much of their work on custom commissions.
  • Most small brands don’t need a custom font. They need a well-made family that covers their scripts and the ₹ sign, a licence that covers apps and ads, and a custom wordmark.

When Air India unveiled its new identity in 2023, people argued about the logo, and almost as loudly about the font. The airline had commissioned its own, Air India Sans, and social media had opinions about both1. Anand Naorem of the Indian type studio Altertype put the reason simply in a later interview: we read text every second of the day, so type carries more of a brand than most people notice1.

Most brands never think about their font beyond “something clean”. The biggest Indian apps and companies think about it a great deal, and a surprising number commission their own. We wanted to know why, so we tested the fonts that small brands most often pick.

We tried it: can popular fonts write Hindi, or even ₹?

How we checked this

On 22 November 2026 we opened ten widely used fonts with the open-source fontTools library and counted how many characters each one maps in four Indian scripts: Devanagari (used for Hindi and Marathi, 128 code points in its Unicode block9), Bengali, Tamil and Gujarati. We also checked for the rupee sign (₹, U+20B9). We tested the regular style of each font as installed on our test machine; versions are listed below. This counts which characters exist, not how well they’re shaped, so a font that passes still needs a visual check.

What we found: nine of the ten fonts had no characters at all in any of the four scripts. Only FreeSans, a free fallback font, covered Devanagari, Bengali and Tamil, and none of the ten covered Gujarati. Two well-known fonts, Open Sans (version 3.003) and Archivo Black (version 1.006), didn’t include the rupee sign in the files we tested.

Why it matters: if your brand font can’t draw a character, the phone or browser quietly borrows one from another font. Your Hindi headline then appears in a typeface you never chose, usually with a different weight, height and personality from the English line above it. Many popular fonts do have separate companion families for Indian scripts, so this isn’t a verdict on any one font. It shows that “we use font X” is not a complete answer for an Indian brand.

Why apps commission their own fonts

1. One personality across every script

A national app might show the same button in English, Hindi, Tamil and Bengali. A custom multi-script family gives all of them the same weight, rhythm and tone, instead of an English brand voice and a set of borrowed fallbacks. Quartz reported that much of India’s type-design industry depends on exactly this kind of custom, multi-script commission from technology and media companies4.

2. Prices are the most-read words in commerce

In a shopping or delivery app, the most-read text is often a price. A custom font can include a rupee sign designed to match, numerals that line up in columns, and figures that stay legible at small sizes on a cheap phone. As our test shows, even the ₹ sign can’t be taken for granted.

3. Licensing at app scale

Fonts are software, and their licences can treat websites, mobile apps, ads and printed packaging differently. A font a company owns removes those questions across every product and market.

4. Being recognisable without the logo

When half the apps on a phone use the same few free sans-serifs, a distinctive font is one of the cheapest ways to make a screen feel like yours, even in a cropped screenshot.

Who makes them in India

India has a small but serious type industry. The Indian Type Foundry, founded in 2009 by Satya Rajpurohit and Peter Biľak, lists clients including Apple, Google, Starbucks, Reliance and Airtel2. Rajpurohit has said the foundry has around 130 retail fonts and about 100 custom ones3, and its Kohinoor family, built to support Indian scripts, is on permanent display at London’s Design Museum8.

Ek Type publishes open-source multi-script families whose users include Indian Railways, Jio IPL, Amul and Britannia, according to its foundry page5. Designer Shiva Nallaperumal’s Oli Grotesk covers nine Indic scripts in the same range of weights as its Latin version6. Studios like Altertype build identities around Indian lettering, such as its work for the auto-parts brand PARTNR drawn from street signage and truck art1. Agencies have made commemorative fonts too, like Rediffusion’s BHARAT, which combines elements from 12 languages7.

What commissioning a font involves

A custom typeface project usually moves through the same stages, whoever the foundry is. Knowing them helps you write a sensible brief, even if you never commission one.

  1. Scope: which scripts, which weights, and which uses: app interface, advertising headlines, packaging or all three. Each script is close to a separate design job, because Devanagari and Tamil letters are built very differently from Latin ones.
  2. Character set: beyond the alphabet, the list should name the rupee sign, numerals in several styles, punctuation for every language you use, and any symbols your app shows next to prices.
  3. Design and testing: drafts get tested at real sizes, on real phones, in real layouts. A font that looks beautiful in a logo can fall apart in a 12 px price label.
  4. Engineering: Indian scripts rely on shaping rules that combine letters and vowel signs correctly. This is specialist work, and it’s where cheap fonts most often fail.
  5. Licensing and ownership: decide who owns the design, where it can be used, and whether agencies and freelancers can install it for your work.

That last point is the one founders most often skip, and the one that causes problems later when a new agency can’t legally use the brand font.

Should a small brand commission one?

Usually not yet. A custom family is a serious commission, and most small brands get more from a good wordmark and a well-chosen, well-licensed family. Here’s how we’d decide.

Your situationWhat we’d do
English-only brand, early stageA well-made family that includes ₹, plus a custom wordmark for the logo
Hindi or regional-language ads a few times a yearPair your Latin font with a matching Indic family, chosen and tested side by side
Bilingual packaging or a multilingual appOne multi-script family designed to work across your scripts, open-source or licensed
National brand, heavy multilingual advertising, own appTalk to a foundry about a custom family or an extension of an existing one

Before you choose any brand font, check five things

  1. Scripts: type a real headline in every language you use, not a pangram, and look at it on a cheap Android phone.
  2. The rupee sign: type ₹499 in every weight you plan to use.
  3. Numerals: check that prices line up in tables and stay readable at 12 px.
  4. Licence: confirm it covers your website, app, ads, social and packaging.
  5. Weights: make sure the Indic companion has the same weights as your Latin font, or your bold Hindi will look nothing like your bold English.

Then write the result into your brand guidelines, including which font to use for each script, so freelancers don’t make the choice for you.

The bottom line

A custom typeface isn’t vanity for a company like Air India or a national app. It’s how they make sure every script, every price and every screen sounds like the same brand. A small brand doesn’t need to commission one, but it should know what its font can’t do. In our test, that was most of India’s scripts and, in two cases, the rupee sign.
