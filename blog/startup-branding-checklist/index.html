The startup branding checklist: what to have ready before you launch

A logo isn’t a brand, but a brand does need more than good intentions. This is the checklist we use with founders to make sure nothing important is missing on launch day.

By Anisha, NMX Labs · Market Research Lead20 August 2026Updated 21 November 20264 min read
Summarise with Google AI (opens Google AI Mode in a new tab)

Quick summary

  • Decide positioning, customer and personality before designing anything.
  • Search trademarks and secure the domain and social handles before you fall for a name.
  • Build a logo system with colour, type, imagery and voice rules, written up as short guidelines.
  • Own every source file and get the IP assigned to your company in writing.

Summary by Anisha. Prefer an AI take? Ask Google AI to summarise it (opens in a new tab).

Founders usually come to us when they need a logo. What they actually need is a set of decisions and files that let everyone, from a freelancer to a marketplace listing team, present the business the same way. Here’s the full checklist. Skip what you don’t need, but skip it on purpose.

1. Foundations: decide before you design

  • One-sentence positioning. Who it’s for, what it does, why it’s different. If you can’t say it in one sentence, no visual identity will rescue it.
  • Your customer, described specifically. Not “millennials”, but who they are, what they buy now and what annoys them about it.
  • Three to five competitors and how each looks, so you can deliberately not look like them.
  • Personality in three words, with what each word does not mean. “Bold, not loud.”
  • Proof points: the facts you can stand behind (ingredients, certifications, numbers).

2. Name and protection

  • Trademark search in your classes before you fall in love with a name. In India, start with the IP India public search and get a professional opinion before you file.
  • Domain secured, plus obvious misspellings if the name is unusual.
  • Social handles reserved on every platform you might use, even the ones you won’t use yet.
  • Pronunciation check across the languages your customers speak. Say it out loud on a phone call.

3. Logo system, not just a logo

  • Primary logo, plus a horizontal and a stacked version.
  • Symbol or monogram that works at 32 pixels (favicon, app icon, profile picture).
  • Colour versions: full colour, one colour, black, and white for dark backgrounds.
  • Clear space and minimum size rules.
  • Files: SVG and PDF for vector use, PNG with transparency for everyday use, and the editable source. Make sure you own the source files; it should be in your contract.

4. Colour

  • Primary and secondary colours, each with HEX and RGB for screens, and CMYK for print. Add Pantone if you’ll print packaging.
  • Accessible combinations: which text colours are readable on which backgrounds.
  • Proportions: how much of each colour a typical layout uses. A palette of six equal colours looks like a palette, not a brand.

5. Typography

  • Headline and body typefaces, with licences that cover your website, app and ads.
  • Fallback fonts for tools where you can’t install yours, such as email and slides.
  • Indic script pairing if you’ll communicate in Hindi or other Indian languages. Choose a Devanagari (or other script) typeface that sits comfortably with your Latin one at launch, not in a panic before your first regional campaign.

6. Visual language

  • Photography direction: lighting, backgrounds, styling, how people appear.
  • Clean packshots of every product on a plain background, high resolution. They’re the raw material for every ad, listing and AI-assisted edit you’ll make.
  • Graphic elements: icons, patterns, shapes, and when to use them.

7. Voice

  • How you sound, with a few “we say / we don’t say” examples.
  • Words you own and words you avoid.
  • Claims you may and may not make, agreed with whoever handles compliance. This saves endless arguments over ad copy.

8. Guidelines that people will actually open

Brand guidelines fail when they’re a 60-page PDF nobody reads. A short guide with the rules above, real examples, and links to the files beats a long one. Ours are intentionally compact for that reason. Writing rules down is also what makes newer brand-aware tools useful, as we covered in our Adobe MAX 2026 breakdown.

9. Launch kit

  • Profile picture and cover images for each social platform.
  • Templates for posts, stories and carousels.
  • Email signature and a simple letterhead or invoice template.
  • A one-page brand overview for partners, marketplaces and press.
  • Website essentials: favicon, Open Graph image for link previews, and a privacy policy.

10. Ownership and handover

  • All source files and font licences in a folder the company owns, not on a freelancer’s drive.
  • Written confirmation that the IP in the work is assigned to your company.
  • One named person responsible for keeping the brand consistent.

The mistakes we fix most often

Designing for the big screen first. Most people will meet your brand as a 40-pixel profile picture or a product tile on a phone. Test the logo and colours at that size before you approve anything.

Too many colours and fonts. Restraint makes a small brand look established. Two typefaces and a tight palette, used consistently, beat a rich system used randomly.

No product photography. Brands spend on a logo and then shoot products on a phone against a bedsheet. Clean packshots do more for ads and marketplace listings than almost any other single asset.

A brand that only the founder can apply. If every post needs the founder’s approval because nobody else knows the rules, the brand isn’t finished. Writing it down is the fix.

Not owning the files. We regularly meet brands that can’t get their own logo source from a former freelancer. Sort out ownership on day one.

How long does this take?

For an early-stage brand, a focused identity project covering the essentials above typically takes a few weeks, most of it spent on decisions rather than drawing.
