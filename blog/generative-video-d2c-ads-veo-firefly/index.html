Generative video for D2C ads in 2026: what Veo and Firefly are actually good for, and what Sora’s shutdown teaches

A year ago the question was which AI video model looks best. In 2026 the better questions are which one will still exist next quarter, which one your lawyer is comfortable with, and which shots it should never be trusted with.

By Neeraj, NMX Labs · AI Design Expert22 November 20266 min readFact-checked 22 November 2026
Summarise with Google AI (opens Google AI Mode in a new tab)

Key takeaways

  • OpenAI closed the Sora app in April 2026 and its video API in September 2026. If your workflow depends on one model, plan for it to disappear.
  • Google’s Veo 3.1 is the active flagship, with a cheaper Lite tier since April 2026. Inside Adobe Firefly, only Adobe’s own model carries Adobe’s “commercially safe” assurances; partner models don’t.
  • Our cost arithmetic shows generation fees are small next to retries and review time. Budget for attempts and people, not seconds of video.
  • Use AI video for B-roll, texture, mood and transitions. Keep it away from your packaging, legible text, real people’s faces and any product claim.

  6. The bottom line

If you run ads for a D2C brand, you’ve probably been pitched AI video at least once this year: a perfect product reveal, a model who never needs a reshoot, a whole campaign made over a weekend. Some of that is real. Some of it falls apart the moment your actual packaging has to appear on screen.

This post skips the demo reels. It covers what changed in 2026, which shots AI video is genuinely good for in a small brand’s ads, what a reel actually costs once you count retries, and a test you can run in one afternoon before committing a budget.

What changed in 2026

Sora is gone

OpenAI told developers in March 2026 that it would discontinue Sora. The consumer app closed on 26 April 2026 and the Sora 2 API was switched off on 24 September 2026, with no replacement announced45. Teams that had built workflows, templates or client promises around it had a few months to move.

The lesson isn’t about OpenAI. It’s that AI video models are products, and products get discontinued. Treat any single model as a supplier you might lose, and keep your prompts, reference images and storyboards in a form you can take to another tool.

Veo got cheaper

Google released Veo 3.1 in October 2025 and added Veo 3.1 Lite, a lower-cost tier for text-to-video and image-to-video, in preview on 2 April 202612. At launch, Veo 3.1 and Sora 2 were among the most expensive video models to run3. The Lite tier changes the maths for short social clips, where 720p is often enough.

Firefly became a marketplace, with different rules inside it

Adobe’s Firefly now offers its own models alongside partner models from companies including Google, OpenAI, ByteDance and Kling8. That’s convenient, but the terms aren’t uniform. Adobe positions output from its own Firefly model as commercially safe and trained on licensed and public-domain content; partner models can be used commercially, but without the same assurances6. Enterprise customers can get indemnity for Firefly-model output specifically7. If a lawyer or a big retail client asks “is this safe to use?”, the honest answer depends on which model made the clip, not which app you opened.

What to do about it

Keep a model-neutral prompt library: a shared document with each prompt, the reference images used, the model and version, the settings and the date. Store the clips you keep with that record beside them. If a model is discontinued or its look changes after an update, you can recreate a shot in another tool instead of starting from memory. It takes five minutes per shot and saves days when a supplier disappears.

What AI video is good for in a D2C ad

Most D2C ads on Meta are 6 to 30 seconds, built from a hook, a few product moments, proof and a call to action. AI video is useful in some of those slots and risky in others. This is our working split for client work.

Use it forKeep it away from
B-roll and texture: steam over chai, rain on a window, fabric moving, spices fallingYour packaging: labels warp, logos melt, text turns to nonsense
Mood and setting: a Kerala backwater at dawn, a Mumbai local at night, a festive living roomLegible text on screen: add text in your editor, never in the generation
Transitions: morphs, reveals and camera moves between real shotsReal people’s faces or voices without written consent, including founders
Concept tests: rough versions of three ideas before you pay for a shootProduct claims: “before and after”, results, ingredients in action
Backgrounds behind real product footage you shot yourselfHands holding the product where the exact label must be readable

The pattern: generate the world around the product, film the product. Your real packaging, shot on a phone in good light, plus an AI-generated setting, beats a fully generated ad where the bottle changes shape between frames.

Whatever you generate, check how the platform will label it. Meta can add an “AI info” label to ads made or edited with AI, and we covered what triggers it in a separate post9.

We tried it: what a 15-second reel really costs

How we checked this

We built a simple cost model for one 15-second Reel made from two 8-second generated clips, trimmed to fit. The rate is the reported Veo 3.1 Lite price of $0.05 per second at 720p4; check your own tool’s current pricing before relying on it. The keep rates are the variable that matters, so we show three.

Keep rateAttempts for 2 usable clipsGeneration costAt ₹88 to the dollar
1 in 36 × 8 s = 48 s$2.40about ₹211
1 in 612 × 8 s = 96 s$4.80about ₹422
1 in 1020 × 8 s = 160 s$8.00about ₹704

What it shows: even at a poor keep rate, the generation bill for one reel is a few hundred rupees. The real cost is the person reviewing 20 clips, writing better prompts, and editing the keepers together. At a ₹500-an-hour freelance rate, two hours of review already costs more than the worst case above.

The formula, for your own tool: cost per reel = clips needed × clip length × price per second ÷ keep rate. Higher-quality tiers multiply the price per second, so the same reel can cost several times more on a flagship model. The exchange rate is an illustrative round number.

A one-afternoon test brief

Before you change your production process, run this test on one real product. It takes about four hours and tells you more than any comparison article, including this one.

  1. Pick one existing ad that performed well, so you have a benchmark.
  2. Write three shot prompts for B-roll only: setting, texture and one transition. Keep the product out of them.
  3. Generate each prompt in two tools, with the same reference image, and log attempts until you have one keeper per shot.
  4. Film the product yourself against a plain background, then cut a new version of the ad that mixes your footage with the AI shots.
  5. Run both versions as a small split test on Meta with the same budget and audience for a week.
  6. Record four numbers: attempts per keeper, total minutes spent, generation cost, and the test result. Those four tell you whether AI video belongs in your process.

So which should a small brand use?

If you…Start withWhy
Already pay for Creative Cloud or FireflyFirefly’s own video modelClearest commercial terms of the options here67
Want the most realistic short clips for socialVeo 3.1, or Lite for volumeActively developed, with a cheaper tier for drafts12
Want to compare several models in one placeA multi-model app, Firefly includedBut check which model made each clip before publishing6
Need a guaranteed look every monthA real shoot, with AI only for backgroundsModels change and disappear5

The bottom line

Generative video is genuinely useful for small brands, just not in the way the demos suggest. It’s a cheap, fast source of B-roll, settings and transitions around a product you filmed yourself. It isn’t a replacement for your packaging, your people or your claims. And as Sora’s shutdown showed, it isn’t a supplier you can depend on forever. Build the workflow so that the model is replaceable, and the brand isn’t.
