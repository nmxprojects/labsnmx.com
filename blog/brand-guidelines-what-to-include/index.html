What goes in brand guidelines, and how to keep them short enough that people use them

Most brand guidelines are long PDFs nobody opens after launch. Good ones answer the questions your team, freelancers and marketplace managers ask every week.

By Anisha, NMX Labs · Market Research Lead3 September 20263 min read
Summarise with Google AI (opens Google AI Mode in a new tab)

Quick summary

  • Write guidelines for the people who make things every week, not for the launch presentation.
  • Cover logo use, colour, type, imagery, voice, claims and templates, with real examples of right and wrong.
  • Ten focused pages with links to files beat a sixty-page PDF.
  • Written rules also make brand-aware AI and resizing tools far more useful.

Summary by Anisha. Prefer an AI take? Ask Google AI to summarise it (opens in a new tab).

The test of brand guidelines isn’t how they look on launch day. It’s whether a freelancer you hired yesterday can make a correct Instagram post without asking the founder. If the answer is no, the guidelines aren’t finished, however beautiful the PDF.

Here’s what we put in guidelines for startups and small brands, and why we keep them short.

Who actually reads them

Think about the people who will open the file: an in-house designer, a performance marketer making ad variations, a marketplace listing manager, a printer, a packaging vendor, a video editor, and increasingly an AI tool that needs your rules written down to follow them. None of them want your brand story in 40 pages. They want answers.

The sections

1. One page on what the brand is

Positioning in one sentence, the customer in a short paragraph, personality in three words with what each does not mean. “Warm, not cute.” This page settles arguments faster than any rule.

2. Logo

  • Every approved version: primary, horizontal, stacked, symbol.
  • Which version to use where, with examples (website header, profile picture, packaging, invoice).
  • Clear space and minimum sizes, in pixels for screens and millimetres for print.
  • Colour versions and which backgrounds each works on.
  • A short “don’t” section with real mistakes: stretched, recoloured, outlined, placed on busy photos.

3. Colour

  • Each colour with HEX, RGB, CMYK and Pantone if you print.
  • Proportions: roughly how much of each colour a typical layout uses.
  • Accessible text and background pairs, so nobody puts light grey text on cream.

4. Typography

  • Typefaces, weights and where each is used, with a simple size scale.
  • Fallback fonts for email, slides and tools where yours can’t be installed.
  • Indic scripts if you communicate in Hindi or other languages, with a tested pairing.
  • Licence notes: what the licence covers, and who to ask before using the font somewhere new.

5. Imagery

  • Photography direction: light, backgrounds, styling, how people appear.
  • Product shots: angles, backgrounds and file locations.
  • Illustration or icon style, if you have one.
  • Your rules for AI-generated imagery: what may be generated and what must be real.

6. Voice

  • How you sound, with “we say / we don’t say” pairs.
  • Words you own and words you avoid.
  • How formal you are in Hindi or regional-language copy, if relevant.

7. Claims and mandatory text

The section most guidelines skip, and the one that saves the most trouble. List the claims you’re allowed to make, the proof behind each, and the ones you must not make. Add standard disclaimers and the exact wording for offers. For packaged products, note the text that must appear on every pack.

“…requires advertisements to be legal, decent, honest and truthful, and not hazardous or harmful…”

Advertising Standards Council of India, The ASCI Code

8. Templates and examples

Real, approved examples of a feed post, a story, an ad, an email header and a marketplace tile. People copy examples far more reliably than they follow rules.

9. Where the files live

Links to the logo folder, fonts, templates and product photography, plus the name of the person who owns the brand and approves exceptions.

How to keep it to about ten pages

  • One idea per page. If a page needs scrolling, split it or cut it.
  • Show, then tell. An example with a short caption beats a paragraph of rules.
  • Link, don’t embed. Files change. Link to the folder instead of pasting every export.
  • Cut the history. The story of how the logo was designed belongs in a case study, not in the guide.
  • Version it. Put a date on the cover and keep a short change log at the back.

Format: PDF, web page or both?

A web page is easier to update and search, and easy to share as a link with freelancers. A PDF is easier to send to printers and packaging vendors. We usually do both from the same content: a simple private web page as the master and a PDF export for vendors.

Keeping them alive

Review the guidelines every six months, and whenever the same question gets asked twice. Every repeated question is a missing rule. If you’re building a brand from scratch, start with our startup branding checklist, which covers the decisions that go into these pages.
